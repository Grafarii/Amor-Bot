## Abstract

Prolonged use of immersive social virtual reality (VR) platforms such as VRChat can reorganize not only memory and identity, but also patterns of everyday behavior. This article proposes the construct of offline avoidance to designate a normative, technology-mediated behavioral pattern in which immersive social VR is used to escape, postpone, or reduce contact with aversive offline contingencies, including social demands, bodily self-presentation, academic or occupational tasks, and affective discomfort associated with physical-world life. Offline avoidance is defined behaviorally by measurable actions: canceling or delaying offline plans, extending VR sessions after distress at headset removal, reducing offline social contact, and arranging daily routines so that immersive VR becomes the dominant reinforced activity. The construct is grounded in avoidance learning, negative reinforcement, escape coping, and research on problematic media use, while remaining distinct from diagnostic categories of addiction and from purely cognitive constructs. Three proposed dimensions are social offline avoidance, task and bodily offline avoidance, and return compulsion after transition distress. Offline avoidance is positioned as the behavioral counterpart to virtual dysphoria and as complementary to virtual confabulation within an immersion-psychology framework. Implications for assessment, counseling, platform design, and empirical operationalization are discussed.

Keywords: offline avoidance; virtual reality; VRChat; avoidance learning; negative reinforcement; immersive social VR; virtual dysphoria; behavioral psychology

## 1. Introduction

Immersive social VR allows users to inhabit customizable avatars, form relationships, and participate in persistent communities for many hours per day. Existing psychological work on these environments has emphasized presence, embodiment, identity disjunction, gender affirmation, and transient depersonalization after exposure. Parallel lines of research address problematic internet and gaming use as patterns of excessive engagement with harmful consequences. What remains insufficiently theorized is a specifically behavioral account of how prolonged immersive social VR comes to function as escape from the physical world.

Heavy users often describe not only preferring VR, but actively arranging life around it: sleeping irregularly to remain online, declining offline invitations, postponing study or work, avoiding mirrors or unmodified voice, and returning quickly to the headset after removal. These reports suggest operant patterns. Offline stimuli become aversive or effortful; immersive social VR provides rapid relief, belonging, and identity-congruent feedback; return to VR is negatively and positively reinforced.

We propose the term offline avoidance to name this pattern. The construct is non-diagnostic and descriptive. It does not claim that immersive social VR is inherently harmful, nor that users who find affirmation, friendship, or safety in VR are pathological. It designates a behavioral organization in which contact with offline contingencies is reduced through immersive engagement, especially under heavy daily use.

This article has four objectives: (a) to provide a behavioral theoretical foundation for offline avoidance; (b) to define the construct and its proposed dimensions operationally; (c) to distinguish it from related constructs, including virtual dysphoria, virtual confabulation, and problematic use; (d) to outline implications and methodological guidelines for empirical investigation.

## 2. Theoretical Foundation

### 2.1. Avoidance Learning and Negative Reinforcement

In behavioral psychology, avoidance refers to actions that prevent or postpone contact with aversive stimuli, while escape refers to actions that terminate ongoing aversive stimulation. Both are typically maintained by negative reinforcement: the behavior increases because it reduces or removes an aversive condition (Dymond & Roche, 2009; Krypotos et al., 2015). Over time, avoidance can become persistent even when the original aversive events are infrequent, because safety behaviors themselves prevent corrective contact with offline contingencies.

Applied to immersive social VR, offline avoidance occurs when entering or remaining in VR reduces aversive offline states such as loneliness, social evaluation, bodily discomfort, boredom, academic pressure, or post-immersion distress. The headset becomes not only a medium of entertainment, but a highly efficient avoidance and escape repertoire.

### 2.2. Competing Reinforcement Densities

Behavior is allocated according to relative reinforcement (Herrnstein, 1970). Immersive social VR can deliver dense, immediate reinforcement: social attention, romance, status, humor, identity affirmation, and continuous novelty. Offline life may offer thinner, delayed, or more effortful reinforcement, especially for users facing isolation, stigma, or limited offline support. Under these conditions, time and behavior migrate toward the richer schedule.

Offline avoidance is therefore not explained by immersion alone. It emerges from an imbalance between offline and immersive reinforcement densities, often aggravated by pre-existing vulnerability rather than created ex nihilo by technology.

### 2.3. Escape Coping and Media Use

Research on media and gaming frequently describes escape motives: using digital environments to avoid real-life problems or negative affect (Kardefelt-Winther, 2014; Melodia et al., 2020). Escape coping predicts intensive use and adverse outcomes when it becomes the primary emotion-regulation strategy. Offline avoidance extends this literature by specifying immersive social VR as a high-embodiment escape context and by emphasizing observable offline withdrawal, not only subjective motive.

### 2.4. Relation to Virtual Dysphoria and Transition Distress

Virtual dysphoria (Cicivizzo, 2026b) describes subjective distress involving loss of belonging to the physical world and discordance between avatar and offline body. Behaviorally, that distress can function as an establishing operation: after headset removal, offline stimuli become more aversive, increasing the probability of return to VR. Offline avoidance thus provides the operant link between virtual dysphoria and maintained heavy use. The two constructs are complementary. Virtual dysphoria is phenomenological; offline avoidance is behavioral.

## 3. Operational Definition of Offline Avoidance

We define offline avoidance as a normative, technology-mediated behavioral pattern characterized by repeated actions that reduce, postpone, or escape contact with offline social, bodily, academic, occupational, or affective contingencies through prolonged immersive social VR use, maintained primarily by negative reinforcement (relief from offline aversiveness) and secondarily by positive reinforcement available in the virtual environment.

Offline avoidance is proposed as a construct describing behavioral organization, not as a diagnostic category. It does not pathologize VR communities, transgender or furry identity exploration, or the pursuit of online belonging.

### 3.1. Three Proposed Dimensions

Social offline avoidance. The user reduces offline social contact, declines invitations, delays family interaction, or substitutes immersive relationships for physical-world social contingencies. The function is escape from social evaluation, loneliness contrast, or low offline reinforcement.

Task and bodily offline avoidance. The user postpones study, work, self-care, or confrontation with the offline body and unmodified voice, remaining in avatar-mediated embodiment where presentation is more controllable. The function is escape from task demand and bodily self-stimulus aversiveness.

Return compulsion after transition distress. After removing the headset, the user rapidly resumes VR when offline discomfort rises. Session endings become unstable; "one more hour" functions as escape from transition aversiveness. This dimension links directly to transition shock and belonging dysphoria.

### 3.2. Differential Criteria

Minimum criteria for research identification:

1. regular immersive social VR use (e.g., several hours per day or most days per week);
2. observable reduction, postponement, or escape from offline contingencies temporally associated with VR use;
3. at least one dimension among social, task/bodily, or return-compulsion avoidance;
4. functional indication that VR use reduces aversive offline states (negative reinforcement), not merely that the user enjoys VR;
5. pattern not fully explained by temporary leisure alone or by a primary psychotic disorder;
6. distinction from affirming identity exploration that does not systematically withdraw from offline contingencies.

## 4. Mechanisms of Offline Avoidance

### 4.1. Escape From Offline Aversive States

Offline life may include criticism, loneliness, gender-related stress, academic failure cues, or bodily dissatisfaction. Immersive social VR can rapidly terminate or attenuate these states. Escape responses that reliably reduce aversiveness are strengthened.

### 4.2. Prevention of Corrective Contact

Persistent avoidance limits exposure to offline situations that could rebuild social skill, bodily tolerance, or alternative reinforcement. The offline world then remains comparatively aversive, sustaining further avoidance. This is a classic avoidance trap applied to immersive technology.

### 4.3. Stimulus Control by Headset and Session Cues

Time of day, notifications, friends online, and the headset itself become discriminative stimuli for immersive engagement. Daily routines reorganize around these cues. Offline tasks lose stimulus control.

### 4.4. Aggravation Model

Offline avoidance does not require that VR invent life problems. Pre-existing anxiety, depression, social exclusion, or identity stress may precede heavy use. Immersive social VR may aggravate behavioral withdrawal by offering a densely reinforced alternative world. The model is aggravation, not pure technological causation.

### 4.5. Interaction With Virtual Confabulation

Virtual confabulation (Cicivizzo, 2026a) concerns mnemonic integration of virtual events into autobiographical reality. Users who avoid offline life may also have fewer competing physical episodic anchors, potentially facilitating source confusion. Avoidance and confabulation may therefore co-amplify under heavy immersion, while remaining conceptually distinct: one is operant, the other mnemonic.

## 5. Forms of Manifestation

Canceling offline plans. Declining gatherings, appointments, or family events to remain in VR.

Session prolongation after distress. Re-entering VR quickly after headset removal due to alienation, boredom, or bodily mismatch.

Sleep and schedule displacement. Shifting sleep to protect immersive social time, reducing offline daytime functioning.

Task postponement. Delaying coursework, job search, or chores while "just one more instance" continues.

Bodily and voice avoidance. Minimizing confrontation with mirror, recordings, or unmodified appearance by remaining avatar-embodied.

Social substitution. Treating immersive relationships as exclusive social reinforcement and allowing offline contacts to extinguish.

## 6. Distinction From Related Constructs

| Construct | Central focus | Relation to offline avoidance |
|---|---|---|
| Classical avoidance learning | Escape/prevention of aversive stimuli | Mechanistic foundation |
| Escape coping in media use | Motive to escape real-life problems | Motivational antecedent; offline avoidance emphasizes behavior |
| Problematic social VR / gaming use | Harmful excess and impaired control | Broader frame; offline avoidance is a specific operant pattern |
| Behavioral addiction models | Compulsion and salience | Related but not equivalent; offline avoidance need not meet addiction criteria |
| Virtual dysphoria | Subjective belonging/body distress | Affective establishing operation for avoidance |
| Virtual confabulation | Memory source integration | Complementary cognitive outcome |
| Offline avoidance | Escape from offline contingencies via immersive VR | Proposed behavioral construct |

## 7. Implications

### 7.1. Clinical and Counseling Psychology

Clinicians may encounter clients whose primary complaint is not "VR addiction," but inability to sustain offline routines. Offline avoidance offers a non-moralizing behavioral language: identify aversive offline contingencies, map escape functions of VR, and rebuild alternative reinforcement offline without attacking identity-affirming uses of social VR.

### 7.2. Assessment

Assessment should prioritize behavioral indicators over moral judgments: hours per day, latency to return after removal, canceled offline plans, sleep displacement, and functional analysis of what VR removes (anxiety, loneliness, bodily discomfort, task demand).

### 7.3. Platform Design and Public Health

Platforms optimized for continuous social reinforcement may unintentionally strengthen offline avoidance. Design options include session-aware breaks, offline reintegration prompts, and tools that support planned endings rather than endless re-entry loops.

### 7.4. Immersion Psychology Framework

Together with virtual confabulation and virtual dysphoria, offline avoidance completes a three-axis sketch of prolonged immersion effects:

- cognitive axis: what is remembered as real;
- identity axis: where and as whom one feels at home;
- behavioral axis: what one escapes and returns to.

## 8. Methodological Proposal for Empirical Investigation

### 8.1. Suggested Design

A correlational study comparing heavy immersive social VR users with light users, after ethics committee approval where required. Measures include structured questionnaires and, when possible, behavioral logs (session length, return latency).

### 8.2. Proposed Measures

Dependent variables: Offline Avoidance Scale (to be developed); subscales for social avoidance, task/bodily avoidance, and return compulsion.

Independent variables: weekly immersive hours; session length; months of regular use.

Covariates: virtual dysphoria scores; depressive and anxiety symptoms; offline social support; problematic VR use screens.

### 8.3. Hypotheses

H1: Heavy immersive social VR use will predict higher offline avoidance scores than light use.

H2: Virtual dysphoria will positively predict return compulsion after transition distress.

H3: Lower offline social support will strengthen the relation between immersive hours and social offline avoidance.

H4: Offline avoidance will correlate with, but remain factorially distinct from, virtual dysphoria and virtual confabulation.

---

## 9. Limitations and Research Agenda

The construct is predominantly theoretical. Related ideas appear in escape-coping and problematic-use literatures, but offline avoidance as an immersive-VR-specific behavioral construct requires scale development and validation. Open questions include: Does avoidance precede heavy use, follow virtual dysphoria, or maintain bidirectional loops? Can offline reinforcement enrichment reduce avoidance without harming affirming VR communities? How should return latency and canceled plans be standardized across users?

Future work should prioritize item generation with heavy VRChat users, ecological momentary assessment around session endings, and longitudinal designs.

---

## 10. Final Considerations

Prolonged immersive social VR can reorganize behavior by offering rapid escape from offline aversiveness and dense alternative reinforcement. Offline avoidance names this pattern without reducing users to addicts or dismissing the genuine social value of virtual communities. Understood alongside virtual dysphoria and virtual confabulation, it suggests that immersive technologies affect not only what people remember and where they feel they belong, but also what they repeatedly escape.

As social VR expands, psychology needs behavioral vocabulary adequate to these contingencies. Offline avoidance is proposed as a contribution to that vocabulary.
